[Written during a Global Service Learning program in Tanzania]

For several decades now, Africa has been regarded as the “cradle of humankind”, but this
was not always the case. At least according to our tour guide, Kivuyo. The beginning of today
was a brief respite from all the hiking and camping – we buckled up into the Land Cruisers for
a two-hour safari to Olduvai Gorge Museum where we explored the site of some of the oldest
human remains ever found.

Upon arriving, the group seemed more excited for the real bathrooms and mirrors (our setup
for the past few days had been more... rudimentary) but that would soon be forgotten and
replaced by wonder and curiosity. After a brief lunch, we were directed to an outdoor
amphitheater that overlooked a stunning expanse of land – the Olduvai Gorge.


Pictures really couldn’t capture its majesty – layers of earth – red, white, and black – lay
exposed in their rock beds, each telling their own stories of flooding and famine, eruption and
ice. Our tour guide, Kivuyo, began to tell us the story of human evolution, both biologically
and technologically. From our early hominid ancestors to genocidal clashes between different
human species, Kivuyo animatedly described scenes of the past as if they were happening
live in the gorge before us. What struck me most about his speech, however, was that he not
only told us the story of human evolution, but also our understanding of it. Kivuyo explained
that before the findings in the Gorge, it was common belief among the scientific community
that Asia was the “cradle of humankind”. It was only after advances in carbon-dating and
DNA-sequencing technology as well as multiple decades of persistent searching that an
archaeologist named Mary Leakey discovered the skull of Zinjanthropus, effectively
disproving the consensus of the time. After his lecture, Kivuyo offered us the incredible
opportunity to walk through the gorge to see and touch the fossils ourselves, which we
eagerly accepted. As we trod over basalt under the blistering sun (see John, dubbed "Arctic
Raider" for extravagant sun-protective outfit), Kivuyo handed us fossilized bones (and fecal matter) that were supposedly over two million years old. It was truly then, as I stood in a pile of ancient volcanic ash, holding a large hunk of fossil that was older than I could ever begin to fathom, I realized how incredible it was, what we were doing.

It was certainly something to ponder during the trek to our next campsite. As we neared the
massive boulder that marked our new home, the brush grew thicker and thornier. Eventually, we were surrounded by spiny bushes from all sides. Being as clumsy as I am, I inevitably snagged my shirt and hair on a plant, tearing some fabric and causing me to grumble for the remainder of the walk. After arriving and setting up tents, our safari guide,Tim, called us out to a corner of the campsite. I grudgingly stumbled over, ducking and hopping to dodge clumps of thorn-trap from above and below. Then, Tim did something that completely changed my mood for the night. He simply leaned into the tree and blew into one of the fruit husks.

After a few anticlimactic seconds, a flood of tiny black ants spilled out of the husk, crawling all over the branches and spines. I stood, transfixed in both horror and awe until Tim began to explain. The spines were an adaptation specific to Africa – a defense mechanism against giraffes, but they adapted back. Eons of evolution granted them numb mouths and thick tongues to overcome the pain. As a response, the tree granted these little ants a home in exchange for an extra line of defense. The ants would crawl into the ears and nose of the giraffe to bite, protecting their colony (and tree, by extension). And so, as Tim explained to us this game of evolutionary ping pong, my perspective on our environment began to shift. The notion of such intricacy in our surroundings replaced my sultriness with a sense of wonder.

After a gorgeous sunset over the ridge, we retreated into our tents. Before I closed the zipper, I spotted two distant firelights – a sign of a Masai encampment. As I settled into my sleeping bag, replaying the day’s scenes in my head, I recalled too my observations of the Masai people. Throughout the previous week, I had made it my intention to interact with the tribe as much as I could, learning their language and numbers and spending a majority of our treks getting to know the kids. As I reminisced on my wonderful interactions with them, I realized that what struck me most about the Masai was their incredible ability to coexist with their surroundings. They derived everything from their land in an impressively low-impact way, and had learned to find peace where we might find immense discomfort. I saw, reflected in this tribe, both the giraffe and the acacia – the great work of evolution collapsed down to the scale of just generations. The way the Masai people adapted to discomfort and learned to peacefully coexist with their environment amazed me and gave me a new perspective to consider. All of a sudden, the daily quibbles of my life in Seattle seemed insignificant, reminding me of how grateful I should be for the luxuries that I have, but not because I believe the Masai are living poorly or primitively. Rather, I have gained insight from a culture that adapts and coexists with discomfort instead of complaining about it, an experience that will change my outlook for the rest of this trip and one that I am immensely grateful for.
