[Written during a Global Service Learning program in Tanzania]

I woke up yesterday morning to find that all but one of my older host siblings had mysteriously
vanished. It struck me as odd, but I assumed they had just left early to fetch water or something
similar. Only over breakfast did I learn that the previous day would be the last time I’d see Anita
and Anceth – likely forever.

They had left for boarding school in a distant city. It was all a bit strange and jarring: no warning, no chance to say goodbye, and no explanation until after the fact. But what intrigued me more was why Jonia hadn’t left with them. That, I’d have to ask later.

I packed up and rushed to school, where we were heading out for a beach day at Lake Victoria
(the “beach” in question was more a pile of rocks and some grass, but it was a refreshing time
nonetheless). During the few hours we had, we were given the opportunity to reflect on our
trips thus far and set intentions for the remainder. My first thought was to get to know my
father better. He was a mysterious figure – rarely at home (he would regularly return at around
2 AM) and a very quiet man. As I pondered what to ask him on our walk home from the lake,
I realized I could talk to Michael – the founder of Twegashe and our guide around Bushasha –
about an interview. I struck up a conversation with him about my father, his life, and our family history. After talking for around ten minutes, I began formulating a list of interview questions. With the intention of wrapping up, I finally asked Michael if there were any questions I might not consider (due to my limited knowledge and perspective) that he thought would be potentially interesting.

After a brief silence, Michael spoke and it was at this moment that I realized just how superficial my understanding of everything had been. Michael posed a question that turned our 10-minute long conversation into one that lasted over an hour and forced me to completely pivot in the direction of my interview – one that ended up unraveling Michael’s past, the mystery of Jonia’s stay, and the history of the Tanzanian education system. In the next section of this blog, I will include the context from this conversation during my account of the interview to provide the fullest perspective I can.

1. Tell me about your childhood and where you grew up.

My father spoke at length, and Michael translated to the best of his ability. Aristides Festo, my father, was born and raised in Bushasha. His childhood was fraught with trouble and misfortune. At the age of just six, he tragically lost his father to illness, leaving his mother to raise him and his five siblings alone. It left a lasting impact, shaping both his childhood and the rest of his life. As one of the older siblings, he was often forced to juggle many extra responsibilities, making school a secondary concern. He spent his days cooking meals, caring for his younger siblings, and selling maize and peanuts at the market. Adding to these burdens, there were family disputes over land, and he often found these issues consuming his thoughts, further hindering his ability to study.

2. What are your thoughts on the education system in Tanzania?

As soon as Michael translated, both men’s expressions darkened. Their dissatisfaction was
apparent before Aristides even began to speak. Michael had already related his experiences
with the education system to me the previous day: he, too, had grown up in Bushasha. The
local school he attended was underfunded and understaffed, with five teachers responsible for 800 students.

By Michael’s time, the teachers had essentially given up. They’d write notes on the board for
students to copy, abandoning them to self-study for the national exams. Michael explained that
the schooling system had no tolerance for failure; if you didn’t pass, you were expelled and
henceforth denied access to education. Beginning as early as second grade and continuing all the way through to university were these “one strike” exams – the very ones, it turned
out, that ended Jonia’s educational journey in 4th grade. To make matters worse, secondary
schools had very limited capacity, and only the top 10 students in each grade would pass
into them, creating a cutthroat atmosphere. Michael explained that he was lucky to make it
through university, but that almost all of his family and friends were not as fortunate; Aristides was an example. Michael noted that the very first thing my father had stated was that the system was “a trap”. He had described how the students are taught in Swahili throughout primary school with English as a single subject, but the exams were only in English, which placed students from public schools at a severe disadvantage compared to those from less affordable private or English-medium schools. A system that privileged the already privileged and neglected those from less fortunate backgrounds. He had also described his frustration with how decisions about education were made by people who had no understanding of the real situation on the ground.

3. What do you want/not want for your kids? What do you want them to achieve?

Aristides’ answer was almost immediate. It was clear he had put a lot of thought into the matter
already. His answer was still fairly simple, however: He wanted his children to understand who
they were, and he believed an education was critical for that. He also wanted them to have
better opportunities than he did so they could live a better life. A straightforward response
that made complete sense, but I probed deeper.

4. How would you feel if one of your kids didn’t make it past an exam?

I was curious whether failing an exam would be a devastating blow to my father or something
he could accept without too much worry. I had a feeling I already knew the answer, but his
reasoning was different from what I expected. Aristides first described how incredibly hard
he worked through day and night to provide for his family – the reason I had rarely seen him
around and why he always arrived home so late. He worked two jobs not only to be able to
put food on our table and a roof over our heads, but also to afford supplies and uniforms for
five of his kids. An incredible mountain of responsibility unfortunately not unlike that of his
childhood. As I listened to Michael’s translation of Aristides’ daily life and duties, I felt intense feelings of admiration, gratitude, and even some guilt rush through me. That even through all of this hardship he and his family were willing to let a stranger take one of the rooms in their house, eat their food, and use their water reminded me how much I wanted to give back to my host family and community. Aristides then explained how failing an exam wasn’t just the end of a child’s education – it represented the nullification of years of his work. Failing an exam meant that his investment in a child’s future was effectively wasted, and that was a painful realization. Aristides mentioned that this was especially hard with Jonia, and he was doing everything he could to make sure none of his other children experienced the same fate.

5. How do you feel about not making it farther in your educational journey? Are you content
with your current life?

Now seemed like the right time to ask this one. It was the one that Michael had posed the
previous day – the one that had shaped this whole interview and the answer to which Michael
had noted he himself didn’t have a grasp on. After Michael had graduated and spent years
teaching, he and his wife Jeanette decided to start Twegashe, an English-medium school in
Bushasha, to offer a higher-quality education than what he had received. They raised funds in
the U.S. to cover tuition, and the community supported the school’s upkeep. Twegashe helped
to alleviate the strain on the local school and improved the quality of education for everyone
in Bushasha. Michael had poured so much into this project, but there was still a lingering
question for him: For those he grew up with, their chance to receive a full education and the
opportunities that came with it had long passed. He didn’t quite understand – had they moved
on? Did they still feel left behind? When I finally asked Aristides about all of this, I was initially met with silence. It was obvious that he was deep in thought, perhaps for minutes. I started to worry I had asked him too much in one question. Have you moved on? Do you feel left
behind? Is there more you want to achieve? Finally, he spoke. The feeling was hard to explain,
it seemed. It was certainly negative, and something he thought about often. From what I could
understand, it was a mix of disappointment and wistfulness. Aristides noted that he had always been a very hard worker – something that I completely believed – and had his childhood been less troubled, he felt that he would’ve made it much farther. He also explained that he often daydreamed of what his life could have looked like if he had finished his education. But when asked whether he was happy with his life now, his response was simpler. He could feed his family, keep them housed, and maintain a job. Michael translated this, and it became clear to me: My father wasn’t necessarily searching for happiness in the way I had assumed. His life was about survival and ensuring that his family had the basic necessities. This realization shifted my perspective entirely.

After the interview, I thanked Michael for his translation. I was deeply grateful for the chance
to engage with my father on such a meaningful level. Up until then, my conversations with
him had been limited by understanding and translation capacity of an 11 year old still learning
English, and I believe understanding and connecting with those who have been vulnerable
enough to accept us into their families on a deeper level is the least we can do. I thanked
him in Kihaya, shook his hand, and grabbed my bag. As I got up to leave, however, Michael
stopped me.

“Wait,” he said. “Your father has a question for you.”

Intrigued, I sat back down. After a moment of translation, Michael chuckled. “He asked you
where you learned to think like this – to ask such mature questions.”

It was my turn to fall silent. I was confused – my questions felt basic to me – but when I
told Michael this, he explained that in many cultures, especially in Tanzania, curiosity isn’t
encouraged. From a young age, kids are conditioned through punishment (often physical) not
to question their parents and do what they say, no questions asked. They are then brought
into schools where this culture is reinforced in their learning – memorizing dates and facts but
not developing skills like critical thinking and curiosity. Michael even mentioned the layout
of classrooms. How the traditional rows and columns of desks impeded collaboration and
discussion.

The three of us ended up chatting for another hour on those benches, delving into Twegashe’s
curriculum, my father’s evolving approach to parenting, and my own background. During that
time, my father also mentioned how grateful he was for my questions. It seemed like I had
given him an opportunity to really reflect on his life thus far and engage with his feelings head on - something rare to come by in a place where people were punished in their childhoods for crying and showing emotion.

As I walked home that evening from what was supposed to be a 30-minute interview turned
into a multiple-hour discussion, I too felt a deep sense of gratitude more than anything. I am
grateful for my home and family in Seattle. The conditions we live in and the life we have built. For my host family and how much they are giving up to support me, previously a complete
stranger to them.

For my Indian heritage and how much it has broadened my perspective and allowed me to
understand things outside the U.S. on a deeper level. For the incredible opportunity to go on
this trip and immerse myself so deeply in another life and culture. And most of all for my
parents and both the education they have provided me with as well as the values and manner
they have raised me in. That they have cultivated my curiosity.
